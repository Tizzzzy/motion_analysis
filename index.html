For new attorneys & law students · currently scoped to Rule 12(b)(6)

Before you file the motion, understand why you're filing it.

Most young attorneys draft a motion because a partner told them to — not because they've worked through what the motion is actually supposed to accomplish. This tool walks you through that thinking first, then helps you map the complaint against the Rule 12(b)(6) pleading standard, element by element.

Why no "likelihood of winning" score? Most legal-judgment-prediction research doesn't demonstrate genuine prediction — it tests models on information taken from the final ruling itself, which the attorney never had when the decision to file actually had to be made (Medvedeva & McBride). So this tool only uses information that exists before a ruling — the complaint's allegations, the claim type, and the Rule 12(b)(6)/Twombly‑Iqbal standard — and reports pleading completeness, not a predicted outcome.

How this works

1
Walk through your mindset Answer a short series of Socratic and client-counseling questions — your purpose, the judge's likely reaction, your opponent's response, and what you'd need to discuss with your client. This is also how you submit your own reasoning, separate from the complaint text.
2
Review your reflection See all of your answers together. This is the thinking that should drive how you evaluate the motion — not the other way around.
3
Run the Rule 12(b)(6) decision tree Paste the complaint's allegations. Work through whether each element (duty, breach, causation, cognizable harm) and the Twombly‑Iqbal plausibility standard are adequately pled, and get follow-up questions wherever you're unsure.
4
Feedback after you decide Coming soon Once a ruling is issued, compare your reasoning against the court's actual analysis — reserved for after the fact, so the ruling never leaks into the initial analysis.
