1. Rule 12(b)(6) Pleading Sufficiency

Four Corners Restriction: Evaluates only well-pleaded facts in the complaint and contracts incorporated by reference.

Twombly & Iqbal Standard: Legal conclusions and formulaic elements are disregarded. Surviving factual allegations must establish a plausible (not merely speculative) entitlement to relief.

7th Circuit Liberal Amendment: Under Runnion v. Girl Scouts (7th Cir. 2015), district courts freely grant leave to amend upon dismissal unless amendment is futile.

Authorities: Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(6); Twombly, 550 U.S. 544; Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662; Ivey v. Transunion, 2022 IL 127903.
2. Rule 56 Summary Judgment Standard
Procedural Notice: Pleading sufficiency is not the same as an evidentiary determination. A Rule 56 motion cannot be assessed solely from the complaint; it requires a developed evidentiary record.

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 56: Summary judgment is appropriate if there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Materiality depends on substantive law; a genuine dispute exists if a reasonable factfinder could rule for the nonmovant.

Evidentiary Inferences: The court views evidence and reasonable inferences favorably to the nonmovant and does not resolve credibility disputes. Factual assertions must be supported by appropriate record citations. Partial summary judgment is possible.

Illinois Breach-of-Contract Issues: Enforceable contract; plaintiff performance or excuse; defendant breach; and resulting damages. Relevant analysis may include interpretation of material terms (e.g. Sec. 11.2 limitation of liability), proof of damages, and supported defenses.

Key authorities: Fed. R. Civ. P. 56; Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317 (1986); Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242 (1986); Swyear v. Fare Foods Corp., 911 F.3d 874 (7th Cir. 2018).
